The math

Your numbers, shown. Every one sourced.

Sculprē sets your calories, macros, and each lift’s next sets from published research, checked against national reference data on how much energy people really use. Here is exactly how, with the sources.

1. Maintenance: what you burn in a day.

Built from its parts, so a lifter who walks 12,000 steps and a desk worker of the same size don’t get the same number.

Maintenance = resting rate × 1.40 + steps + training

Resting rate

From your lean mass when we know your body fat (Cunningham’s equation), else from your height, weight, age, and sex (Mifflin-St Jeor, the equation reviews rate most reliable). A DEXA report’s RMR is calculated by the scanner, so we use your lean mass instead, unless you mark it as measured by a breath test.

× 1.40

Digesting food and the moving you do between steps and workouts. We checked it against the National Academies’ 2023 equations for daily calorie burn, built from 5,456 measurements of adults using the doubly labeled water method. Across our sample members, our estimate lands about 4% under those equations on average, inside their own margin of error.

Steps and training

Steps at 0.4 calories per kg of body weight per 1,000, the cost of walking above resting. Each session from your usual week at its effort, using the 2024 Compendium of Physical Activities: a legs or back lift, for example, burns about 4 × your weight in kg per hour above resting.

Then we measure yours.

Every two weeks, once you’ve logged 12 of 14 days and weighed in 10 times, Sculprē compares what you ate with how your weight trended and sets your maintenance from that. Two DEXA scans six or more weeks apart work too: fat counts as 9,400 calories a kg and lean tissue as 1,800 (Hall, 2008). Each new measurement is blended 70/30 with the last, so one noisy fortnight can’t swing your plan. Because it’s measured in the calories you actually log, it already accounts for anything you tend to miss.

2. Your pace: how fast to change.

A pace is a share of your body weight a week, turned into calories at 3,500 a pound. Nothing goes faster than 1% a week.

GoalPaceA weekWhy
Lose fatEasy0.35%A gentler cut, below the studied range
Lose fatSteady0.5%The low end of 0.5 to 1% for lean, trained people (Helms, 2014)
Lose fatAggressive0.75%Near the pace that kept lean mass in athletes (Garthe, 2011)
BuildSlow0.25%Experienced lifters (Iraki, 2019)
BuildSteady0.5%Newer and intermediate lifters (Iraki, 2019)

Faster than 0.75% a week while lean (15% body fat for men, 23% for women, or under) shows a warning before you save it.

3. Your macros: protein first.

Protein and fat are set first and stay the same every day. Carbs carry the rest, or fat does if you eat keto or low carb.

ProteinWith your body fatWithout itSources
Losing fat or recomp2.9 g per kg of lean mass2.4 g per kg of body weightHelms, 2014; Longland, 2016; Vargas-Molina, 2026
Maintaining or building2.4 g per kg of lean mass2.0 g per kg of body weightISSN, 2017; Morton, 2018

Fat

0.36 g per pound of body weight, kept between 20 and 35% of your calories on balanced days (the Dietary Reference Intakes’ range). Protein never goes past 40% of your calories.

Floors

No day goes under your resting rate, 1,500 calories (men) or 1,200 (women), 30% under maintenance, or 60 g of carbs on a balanced plan. Lean people on a cut also never go under 30 calories per kg of lean mass plus the day’s training, or more than 500 a day under maintenance (IOC, 2023; Murphy and Koehler, 2022).

4. Your goal sets the direction.

GoalCaloriesProtein
Lose fatA deficit at your paceThe fat-loss rate
RecompMaintenanceThe fat-loss rate
MaintainMaintenanceThe maintaining rate
Get strongerMaintenance, or a small surplus if you want size tooThe building rate

Strength gains came about equally with and without a calorie deficit in the pooled trials (Murphy and Koehler, 2022), so Get stronger starts at maintenance.

5. Your next sets: earned, not guessed.

Each lift moves through a rep range, then adds weight, following the American College of Sports Medicine’s progression stand and the NSCA’s coaching standards.

RuleHow it worksSource
Add weightAfter two sessions in a row at the top of the range, two extra reps on your last set two sessions running, or every set with 3+ reps to spareACSM, 2009; NSCA 2-for-2
How much5% on compound lifts, 2.5% on isolation lifts, rounded to your plates, never more than 10%. A jump that big (light dumbbells, kettlebells) is earned with extra reps firstACSM, 2009; NSCA
Between jumpsOne more rep per set; a session near your limit repeats insteadPlotkin, 2022
Backing offOnly after two sessions with a set under the range: 10% on squats, deadlifts, and other lower-body compounds, 5% on the rest, with one set fewerNSCA
StuckThe same weight × reps three times adds about 2.5%; stuck again soon after earns a lighter sessionBell, 2023; Rogerson, 2024
RecoveryReadiness can make a day easier, never harderOur call: ACSM, 2026 found too little evidence for pushing harder
Weekly volume10 to 20 hard sets per muscle a week for size, counting today’sACSM, 2026; Schoenfeld, 2017
Rep ranges3 to 6 on big lifts for strength (6 to 10 while a lift is new to you), 6 to 10 or 10 to 15 for sizeACSM, 2009 and 2026
RecordsOnly sets of 10 reps or fewer count toward a PRReynolds, 2006
Rest3 minutes after big lifts, 90 seconds after isolation lifts, until you pick your ownACSM, 2009; Schoenfeld, 2016

We ran these rules through 12 simulated weeks for a beginner and a trained lifter: no jump ever passed 10%, and the beginner’s squat kept pace with their strength instead of backing off while they were improving.
